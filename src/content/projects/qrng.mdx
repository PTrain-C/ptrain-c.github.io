## What it is

This is a random number generator I'm building from the qubit up. The random bits come from two places: qubits measured on IBM Quantum hardware, and a ring oscillator running on the FPGA itself. Both streams go into a Digilent Basys 3 (Artix-7), where hardware I'm writing checks that the source hasn't failed, strips out bias, and compresses the bits into output that is very close to uniform. That's the same basic structure commercial QRNGs use. The output goes two places: back to my laptop over UART so I can run statistical tests on it, and into a Magic 8-Ball on the board's 7-segment display, which is the demo.

The bigger reason for the project is digital design. Most of my work so far is analog and PCB design, and the silicon internships I'm going after (ASIC design and verification) ask for RTL, testbenches, assertions, clock domain crossing, synthesis, and timing. Every block in this pipeline was picked to cover one of those. The quantum part is what makes it more than another generic CPU core.

## What makes it quantum

A normal computer can't make a truly random number. Software generators are deterministic, so the same seed gives the same sequence every time. A qubit doesn't work that way. Start one in $|0\rangle$ and put it through a Hadamard gate:

$$
H|0\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\big(|0\rangle + |1\rangle\big)
$$

When it's measured, each outcome shows up with probability equal to its amplitude squared (the Born rule):

$$
P(0) = P(1) = \left|\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right|^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}
$$

Nothing about the qubit before the measurement decides which one you get. There's no hidden value we just can't see; the result doesn't exist until it's measured. Put a Hadamard on each of three qubits and you get eight outcomes that are all equally likely, which is exactly one Magic 8-Ball answer:

$$
H^{\otimes 3}|000\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{8}}\sum_{x=0}^{7}|x\rangle
$$

Real hardware isn't ideal, though. Readout errors and relaxation (a qubit in $|1\rangle$ decaying back to $|0\rangle$) push the bits away from 50/50, and that's the reason the FPGA side needs health tests and an extractor at all. A couple of rules I'm sticking to:

- Qiskit's Aer simulator is pseudorandom. It's useful for checking the circuit, but its output gets labeled that way and never counts as quantum entropy.
- IBM's free plan only gives a limited amount of runtime a month, so the bits get collected in batches, saved to files, and replayed to the board over UART instead of pulled live for every question.

The ring oscillator is the classical comparison. An odd number of inverters in a loop never settles, and thermal noise makes its timing jitter, so sampling it gives physical randomness that isn't quantum. Running both sources through the same processing and comparing them is the experiment at the end.

The Toeplitz extractor is the one I'm most interested in. It computes

$$
y = T\,x \pmod 2, \qquad T_{i,j} = s_{\,i-j+n-1}
$$

where $x$ is $n$ raw bits, $y$ is $m$ output bits with $m < n$, and every diagonal of the $m \times n$ matrix $T$ is constant. Each output bit is just the XOR of some of the input bits. Since the diagonals are constant, the whole matrix comes from $n + m - 1$ seed bits instead of $mn$, so the hardware only has to hold a shift register, not a full matrix. I'll build a serial version first, then a pipelined one, and compare area against throughput.

A control and status register block on an AXI4-Lite subset ties it together. It holds the source select, the health test limits, alarm and sample counters, and the extractor seed.

**Verification.** Every block gets self-checking cocotb tests on Verilator, with Python reference models and scoreboards. On top of that: random backpressure on every handshake, SystemVerilog assertions, formal proofs in SymbiYosys on the FIFO and arbiter, functional and code coverage, and one full UVM environment. Lint runs with zero warnings allowed.

**Implementation.** On the FPGA side that means XDC constraints (with the two clocks declared asynchronous), timing closure in Vivado, reports on Fmax, utilization, and worst slack, and debugging with Vivado's ILA over JTAG. Then the health tests, debiaser, and extractor go through OpenLane 2 on SKY130 for synthesis, static timing, and place and route reports. That's an ASIC flow, not a tapeout, and the ring oscillator stays out since it isn't portable.

The tests cover:

- One byte, then six bytes back to back with no idle gap between them. `valid` pulses once per byte, 86.8 µs apart.
- A bad stop bit gives exactly one `framing_err` pulse and no `valid`, and the next byte is received normally.
- Glitches of 1, 50, 200, and 434 cycles are all ignored. I measured where the cutoff actually is: 434 cycles is rejected and 435 is accepted, because START samples exactly 434 cycles after the line falls and the two synchronizer delays cancel out.
- A reset in the middle of a byte drops that frame cleanly.
- A bad stop bit running straight into the next start bit. Low into low isn't an edge, so nothing is received.

The last two taught me something about UARTs in general. After a reset or lost sync, any 1 to 0 inside later data looks like a start bit, so the receiver can lock onto the wrong edge and swallow the next real byte. That's just how a UART behaves, not a bug, but it changed how I wrote those tests. A possible upgrade later is 16x oversampling with a 3-sample majority vote.

Around that:

- **CI.** GitHub Actions runs lint (Verilator `-Wall` and Verible) and the full cocotb suite on every push. Green since November 3.
- **Toolchain.** The open-source flow (Verilator, Yosys, SymbiYosys, Verible) runs in WSL2, and Vivado 2026.1 runs natively on Windows for synthesis. The build and programming scripts are ready for the first board build.
- **UART transmitter.** The spec is done: a start pulse and `busy` handshake, a shift register, and `tx` driven straight from a flip-flop, since a glitch on `tx` would look like a start bit to the receiver on the other end.
- **Quantum side.** I'm writing the 8-ball circuit in Qiskit now. My IBM Quantum account is set up and three backends (ibm_fez, ibm_kingston, ibm_marrakesh) are reachable. It runs on Aer first, then on real hardware through `SamplerV2`.
